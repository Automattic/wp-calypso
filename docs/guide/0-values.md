# Development Principles

These principles seek to provide a framework for making better decisions in the complex work of building products. They acknowledge the tensions we face daily—between speed and quality, between learning and shipping, between craft and complexity. They capture pitfalls and think further about the outcomes of our work. They explore how velocity without direction leads us astray, how learning without improvement becomes an excuse, and how beauty emerges through thoughtful reduction. Most importantly, they recognize that great product work requires both urgency and patience—the drive to make work real and the discipline to make it right.

## 1 — Do not optimize for making your job easier if it makes the user experience worse

User needs must guide our decisions. We will often face situations where we craft solutions that make our work simpler while making users’ lives harder. This happens gradually, through small choices that prioritize our convenience. When noticing this drift, pause and rethink. It’s likely we haven’t thought through a problem well enough. Articulating what would be best for the user can bring a problem back to reality from the clouds of abstraction. Their experience is our true north.

## 2 — Do not work on something until you understand why you are working on it

We can only reflect on the impact of our work if we can explain why we are doing it. Understanding what guides our efforts is important to remain focused. Reality tends to move, and focus needs to be corrected. Thoughtless inertia can leave us adrift. Without clear intent, motion masquerades as progress.

## 3 — Do not mistake shortcuts for cutting the journey short

Every shortcut promises a faster arrival but could end somewhere else. True velocity comes from moving quickly on the right path. Instead, we rationalize detours as worthwhile iterations. The person that takes one step at a time on the correct path will eventually arrive at their destination. The person looking for constant shortcuts to avoid the effort of walking can easily become stranded. All too often we rationalize incomplete solutions as stepping stones, when they’re really diverging paths. True progress requires solving core problems, not circumventing them. Bad shortcuts are often dead ends.

## 4 — Do not glorify failure under the guise of learning

There is a difference between the practice of improving based on correcting mistakes and the gradual indifference that settles when we become used to them. It is not uncommon for bad decisions to go unexamined. Those who never make mistakes will hardly improve, but those who internalize mistakes are sure to keep repeating them. It’s the difference between learning from mistakes and making mistakes a learned habit. Excellence is not an act but a practice.

## 5 — Work is invisible until it is real

Great embarrassment is the prelude to great work. Our ideas remain shadows until they meet reality. What we deliver matters more than what we hoped to deliver. Being embarrassed by our work means we see its potential; remaining embarrassed means we failed to act on it. It’s the difference between being embarrassed because we care or being embarrassed because we didn’t. The speed of our iteration reflects the urgency of our care. Our judgement should always be ahead of our work; that’s how we improve. Never remain complacent with what exists today.

## 6 — Beauty also lives in what we take away

There is a rhythm to creation that requires diving in and stepping back. Simplicity is not granted but discovered through ingenuity and reflecting over the essence of things. It works in layers, from the broad strokes to the finest details. Try to reason in a way that allows solving multiple problems at once. Avoid starting things anew when there is an opportunity to improve what is already there. Try to leave things in a better state than you found them. Even the best ideas can wither from poor execution. To create beauty is to think tastefully.
